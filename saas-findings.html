What the SaaS dashboard told me

I built a growth dashboard for a fictional subscription software company. This is my plain-English read of what it shows, and where I think the story is thinner than it looks.

The data is simulated. I wrote a script (seed 42) that generates 5,486 signups, about 55,000 events and 1,469 subscription changes from January 2025 to June 2026. Some patterns below are ones I built in on purpose, so they show the pipeline works, not that anything is true about real customers.

The funnel: most people leave before they start

Of 5,486 signups, 3,059 (56%) got through onboarding, and 1,090 (20%) went on to pay. Put another way, about 36 of every 100 people who activate end up subscribing. The biggest single loss is before activation: 44% of signups never do anything meaningful.

Signups themselves grew steadily, from 226 in January 2025 to 440 in June 2026, which is roughly what I set up (about 4% growth a month).

Referral and organic signups are the best. Social is the weakest, and paid search is the one I would look at first: it is the second-largest source (27% of signups) but a signup from it is worth about a quarter less in new MRR than an organic one, and about 37% less than a referral.

One honest caveat. Once someone activates, every channel converts to paid at about the same rate (33 to 38%). So the entire gap between channels comes from activation. That is because of how I wrote the generator, and in a real company I would want to test whether it holds. Company size showed no real difference either (19 to 23% paid across all four bands).

Retention falls off a cliff, then flattens

Looking at the first ten monthly cohorts, about 66% of a cohort is still doing something in month 1, 29% in month 3 and 12% in month 6. Individual cohorts bounce around a few points (month 3 ranges from 24% to 38%) with no cohort standing out as clearly better or worse.

The split that matters is paying versus not. At month 3, 64% of people who subscribed are still active, against 12% of those who never did. Subscribers are far stickier, which is no surprise, but it is a useful number to point at when arguing that activation work pays for itself.

Revenue: growing, with a leaky bucket

Monthly recurring revenue went from $1,555 in the first month to $61,739 by June 2026. Over the period the company added $93,290 in new MRR and lost $31,551 to cancellations, so about a third of everything it won it gave back.

379 of the 1,090 subscribers cancelled (35%). Churn is front-loaded: half of cancellations came within about three months of subscribing, and three quarters within six. The plan with the highest churn is Starter (36%), with Growth and Scale at about 33%.

Growth slowed a little late on. April 2026 was the lowest month for new MRR since August 2025 ($4,683) while cancellations hit a high of $3,921, so net new MRR was only about $760.

Plans: Scale is small but heavy

54% of subscribers chose Starter ($29), 34% Growth ($99) and 12% Scale ($299). Scale is one subscriber in eight but brings in 42% of all new MRR. Losing a handful of Scale accounts moves the revenue line much more than losing the same number of Starter accounts.

What I would do with this

If this were a real company, I would work on two things. First, get more people through onboarding, especially from paid search and social. Second, find out why people cancel in the first three months, since that is where most of the leakage is. I would also move some paid search budget toward referrals if the pattern held up in real data.

All figures in this page were computed directly from the project's CSV files with the same definitions as the SQL views (activation and paid conversion are share of signups; retention is any event in the month after signup).
